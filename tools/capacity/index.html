Formulas and conventions

tau = min(1, T × k / 242) share of the portfolio traded one way per rebalance trade_i = A × w_i × tau CNY, for portfolio size A and weight w_i participation_i = trade_i / (d × ADV_i) d = days allowed to work the trade capacity = min_i c × d × ADV_i / (w_i × tau) c = participation cap impact_i = Y × sigma_i × sqrt(participation_i) share of trade_i (square-root law) annual drag = 2T × Σ_i w_i × impact_i share of the portfolio per year, grows with sqrt(A) budget size = A at which annual drag = budget share × gross alpha net alpha = gross alpha − annual drag

T is annual one-way turnover: 8 means the whole portfolio is bought 8 times and sold 8 times a year. Trades are spread across holdings by weight, as when a strategy replaces names at the same weights. The factor 2 counts the buy and the sell. ADV is average daily traded value in CNY, for example the 20-day average; sigma is daily return volatility. Weights are normalised to sum to one. Fees and stamp duty are not included: use the A-share trading cost calculator for those and add them to the impact drag.

What the numbers say

  • The smallest, heaviest-weighted names set the capacity. The participation limit is a minimum across holdings, so one illiquid stock at full weight can halve the capacity of the whole book. Capping weights by liquidity raises capacity at once.
  • Impact usually binds first. At a 5% participation cap, the square-root law with Y = 0.7 and 2.5% daily volatility costs about 39 bp per trade; at 8 round trips a year that is 6.3% of the portfolio. Few strategies can give up that much, so their real capacity is the impact-budget size, well below the participation capacity.
  • Cost grows with the square root of size. Four times the money doubles the impact cost per year; the net alpha falls slowly at first and then quickly.
  • Turnover and patience are the levers. Halving turnover halves the drag at any size. Spreading trades over more days raises the participation capacity in proportion and lowers the impact per trade, at the price of trading on older signals.
  • This is an estimate, not a fill. Real impact depends on the time of day, the order type, other traders doing the same thing, and limit-up and limit-down days when a stock cannot be traded at all.

FAQ

What is strategy capacity?
The largest amount of money a strategy can run before trading it costs too much. Two limits matter: how much of each stock's daily trading volume you can take without moving the market (the participation cap), and how much return the market impact of your own trades eats. The smaller of the two is the practical capacity.
How does this calculator estimate capacity?
Each rebalance trades a share of the portfolio equal to annual one-way turnover x days between rebalances / 242, spread across the holdings by weight. The participation capacity is the largest portfolio at which no stock's trade exceeds the cap (for example 5%) of its average daily traded value over the days allowed for trading. The impact cost of each trade follows the square-root law: cost = Y x daily volatility x square root of (trade / daily traded value).
Worked example: 30 stocks with 50 million CNY of daily turnover each
Equal weights, one-way turnover 8 times a year, rebalancing every 10 trading days and trading each rebalance within one day. Each rebalance trades 33% of the portfolio, so each stock trades 1.1% of the portfolio. At a 5% participation cap the capacity is 0.05 x 50 million / 0.011 = about 227 million CNY. At that size, with Y = 0.7 and 2.5% daily volatility, impact costs about 6.3% a year; impact reaches 3% a year at about 52 million CNY. For most small and mid-cap strategies the impact budget binds well before the participation cap.
What participation rate is safe?
There is no single number. Many practitioners keep a single day's trade below 5% to 10% of a stock's average daily volume, and lower for strategies that trade at the open or close or that many others trade at the same time. Spreading a trade over more days raises capacity in proportion but delays the signal.
What value of Y should I use?
Studies of large institutional order sets put the coefficient roughly between 0.3 and 1 when volatility is daily and size is measured against daily volume. Calibrate it to your own fills if you have them: compare the average price you got with the price when the order started, against the square root of your participation.
