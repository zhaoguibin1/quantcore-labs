Complex vs Simple RESEARCH NOTE

A multi-signal stock-selection model for A-shares showed a 21% annual return in its backtest. How much of that needed the model? We rebuilt the portfolio from the three plain styles it turned out to hold, ran both through the same engine with the same costs, and fixed the pass mark before looking. The extra signals did not clear it.

Key findings

  1. The model was mostly three known styles. Its holdings sat around the 74th percentile of the market on cheapness, the 68th on low volatility and the 28th on trading activity, after adjusting for company size. On profitability it was average, and on gross margin and leverage it was worse than average.
  2. A plain version came close. An equal-weight score of value, low turnover and low volatility, run through the same engine, returned 19.8% a year against 21.3% for the full model from 2018-07 to 2026-11, with a higher Sharpe ratio (1.03 against 0.95) and a smaller worst drawdown.
  3. The gap was not significant. The full model was ahead by +2.0% a year with a t-statistic of 0.63. The pass mark fixed in advance was +2.0 points with t ≥ 2 and a lead in both halves; it led by +8.8% a year in the first half and trailed by 4.1% in the second.
  4. Its edge was concentrated in one period. The model was 44% ahead of the simple version by August 2022, built mostly from 2019 to 2021, and by November 2026 the lead was down to 11%. That is the pattern to expect when signals are chosen on the same history they are tested on.
  5. Turnover cost was the clearest difference. The full model traded 8.6 times its capital each way per year against 1.6 for the simple one, which cost about 2.3 percentage points a year more in fees and slippage.

Setup

  • The model. A multi-signal composite score used to run a concentrated long-only A-share portfolio of a few dozen stocks, rebalanced every two weeks. Its signals are not described here; the test treats it as a black box.
  • The simple model. Three plain characteristics, each neutralised against industry and company size and converted to a cross-sectional z-score, then averaged with equal weight: value (earnings, book value, sales and operating cash flow relative to market value), low turnover (20-day average turnover) and low volatility (60-day return volatility).
  • Same engine. Both scores went through the identical backtest: the same investable universe, number of holdings, rebalancing dates, trading rules for price limits and suspensions, and the same cost model for commission, stamp duty and slippage.
  • Criteria fixed in advance. The complex model counted as adding value only if it beat the simple one by at least 2 percentage points a year with a t-statistic of at least 2 on daily return differences, and was ahead in both halves of the sample (to June 2022 and after).
  • Sample. Daily returns from 2018-07-03 to 2026-11-13, 2030 trading days.

What we take from it

  • Benchmark a model against its own tilts. Beating an index is easy for a portfolio of cheap, quiet stocks in a market that rewarded them. The useful hurdle is a plain portfolio with the same tilts.
  • Fix the pass mark first. With the criteria written down, a model that is "+2.0% a year" cannot be talked into a success.
  • Complexity has a running cost. Extra signals that change the ranking every two weeks buy turnover. Here that cost was about 2.3 points a year, more than the model's whole lead.
  • Look at when the edge was earned. A lead built in the years the signals were chosen on, and lost after, is the signature of fitting the past.
  • Simple is not proven either. The three styles were picked after seeing the model's holdings, and all three did well in A-shares over these years. A plain version earns a place as the comparison, not as a forecast.

Limitations

  • Both models are in-sample: the complex one was built on this history, and the simple one was chosen after seeing the complex one's exposures.
  • One market, one period, one portfolio construction. Different holding counts or rebalancing could change the turnover gap.
  • The benchmark for excess returns is an equal-weighted index of investable stocks before costs.
  • Backtest results, not live trading. Past results do not indicate future results.

FAQ

Does this mean simple factor models are better?
No. Both models were built with knowledge of the same history, and the simple one was assembled after looking at what the complex one held. The test shows that, on this history, the complex model's extra signals did not add return a simple version could not get, net of costs. It says nothing about which will do better next.
Why compare against three styles and not against an index?
An index comparison credits the model with every style tilt it happens to have. A strategy that is mostly cheap, quiet, low-volatility stocks should beat the index in a market that rewards those traits. The question that matters for the extra complexity is whether it beats a plain portfolio with the same tilts.
What would have counted as success?
Fixed before the results: the complex model had to beat the simple one by at least 2 percentage points a year with a t-statistic of at least 2, and be ahead in both halves of the sample. In the regression, the unexplained return had to be at least 2% a year with t of at least 2.
Can I run the same checks on my own strategy?
Yes. The holdings exposure step is available as the Style Exposure Analyzer and the per-signal step as the Factor IC Analyzer on this site. The head-to-head test needs your own backtest engine, run twice with only the score changed.
