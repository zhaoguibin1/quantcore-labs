US Sector Residual Mean Reversion After 2020 RESEARCH NOTE

Sector-ETF residual mean reversion is the textbook statistical-arbitrage strategy for US stocks: buy what fell against its sector, short what rose, hedge the sector. We ran the published rules without tuning, first on three sectors and then on eight others it had never seen, with realistic costs and without counting interest on cash as strategy return. From 2016 to 2020 there was a small edge. From 2021 to 2025, after costs, there was none.

Key findings

  1. Out of sample, the edge was gone after 2020. On eight sectors not used in development, the main version earned a Sharpe ratio of 0.63 in excess of cash before costs in 2016–2020 and 0.03 in 2021–2025. With 2 bp a side and 0.5% borrow it was 0.36 and −0.29: about +4.0% a year over cash, then −4.2%.
  2. Trading less did not rescue it. A stricter entry (1.75 instead of 1.25 standard deviations) halved turnover from 142 to 72 times capital a year and kept more of the gross edge, but after costs it still fell from 0.40 to −0.14.
  3. Interest on cash hides the decline. A market-neutral book earns T-bill interest on its capital. Counted as return, it lifts the main version's full-period Sharpe after costs from 0.05 to 0.21, because from 2023 to 2025 T-bills paid 4–5% a year.
  4. The second half was one very bad year and two weak ones. After costs and including cash, the main version lost 20.4% in 2022, trailed T-bills in 2023 (−0.2% against 4.9%) and 2024 (4.7% against 5.2%), and beat them in 2025 (11.2% against 4.2%). The development sectors had a different bad year (2025). Year-to-year noise is large.
  5. The drop is real after costs but not proven as a break. A five-year Sharpe ratio has a standard error of roughly 0.45, so a fall from 0.63 to 0.03 is about one standard error. The data say there was nothing left after costs from 2021 to 2025; they cannot say it will stay that way.

Setup

  • Rules, fixed in advance. Simplified Avellaneda and Lee (2010). For each stock, regress 60 days of daily returns on its SPDR sector ETF; fit a mean-reverting AR(1) to the cumulative residual and keep only fits with a mean-reversion time under 30 trading days; compute the s-score. Buy below −1.25, sell above −0.50; short above +1.25, cover below +0.75. No new entry after a residual move beyond 4 standard deviations on that day or the day before, as a proxy for news. No parameter search.
  • Portfolio. Each new position is 2% of equity; each sector's net beta is hedged daily with its ETF. Signals at the close, trades at the next open. About 93 names held on average in the main version, gross exposure about 2.4 times capital.
  • Development and out-of-sample universes. Development: 141 stocks in energy, financials and technology (XLE, XLF, XLK). Out of sample: 280 stocks in materials, communication services, industrials, consumer staples, real estate, utilities, health care and consumer discretionary, filtered to price above 5 USD and 60-day median traded value above 20 million USD.
  • Costs. Three settings per side on traded value, stocks and ETFs: none; 2 bp with 0.5% a year on short value for borrow (close to a low-cost broker for liquid large caps); 5.5 bp with 3% borrow.
  • Cash. Out-of-sample runs credit equity with the return of a 1–3 month T-bill ETF. Results are shown excluding that interest, which is the return in excess of cash. The development runs credit no interest.
  • Sample. Daily, January 2016 to December 2025, split into two halves fixed in advance. Out-of-sample results were run on two independent commercial price feeds; the second is shown below.

Reference: Avellaneda, M. and Lee, J.-H. (2010), Statistical arbitrage in the US equities market, Quantitative Finance 10(7), 761–782.

Where the money came from

  • A few names did most of the work. In the main out-of-sample run, the ten best stocks produced 70% of the stock-leg profit before costs; 156 stocks made money and 122 lost.
  • Sectors differed widely. Health care and consumer discretionary carried the book; utilities lost the most. In development, technology produced most of the profit. A strategy whose edge depends on which sectors are in it is fragile.
  • Costs are the whole story at this turnover. At 142 times capital a year, each basis point per side costs about 1.4% a year. At 5.5 bp and 3% borrow, even the development sectors lost money in both halves.

Why it might have faded

Not tested here; these are the usual explanations, listed so that a follow-up can test them.

  • Crowding. The rules have been public since 2010 and are cheap to run. More capital chasing the same reversals shrinks them.
  • Trends inside sectors. After 2020, moves within sectors were often driven by themes that kept going (rates in 2022, weight-loss drugs in health care, AI in communication services). A residual that keeps drifting is a loss for a reversal strategy.
  • Sector ETFs as hedges. Cap-weighted sector ETFs became dominated by a few large stocks, so a single ETF beta hedges the typical member less well.
  • Luck. With a standard error of about 0.45 on each five-year Sharpe, 2016–2020 may simply have been a good draw.

What we take from it

  • Report market-neutral returns in excess of cash. After 2022, T-bill interest alone makes a flat strategy look like a 4–5% earner.
  • Test on names the rules were not developed on. The development sectors held up better after 2020 than the eight new ones.
  • Check the cost break-even first. A strategy that trades 70 to 140 times its capital a year needs a gross edge of several percent just to pay 2 bp a side.
  • Split the sample before looking. Halves fixed in advance showed the decline at once; a single full-period number would have shown a modest positive Sharpe.

Limitations

  • Survivorship bias: the universe was compiled from recent sector membership, not historical constituents, and delisted stocks are closed at their last price with no further return. This most likely flatters the results.
  • Current sector classification applied to the whole history.
  • Flat cost and borrow rates; real borrow for hard-to-borrow names is higher and sometimes unavailable. No market-impact model.
  • One set of published rules, simplified; no parameter search, so a tuned version could look different in both halves.
  • Backtest results, not live trading. Past results do not indicate future results.

FAQ

Did sector residual mean reversion stop working after 2020?
In this replication, yes after costs: on eight sectors it was never fitted on, the strategy earned a Sharpe ratio of about 0.36 in excess of cash from 2016 to 2020 and about −0.29 from 2021 to 2025 with 2 bp a side and 0.5% borrow. Before costs the second half was close to zero (0.03). Five years is short, though, and the drop is within about one standard error, so the data cannot separate a lasting break from a bad stretch that included 2022.
What is sector residual mean reversion?
A statistical-arbitrage strategy described by Avellaneda and Lee (2010). Each stock's daily return is regressed on its sector ETF; the cumulative residual is modelled as a mean-reverting process, and the strategy buys stocks whose residual is unusually low and shorts those where it is unusually high, hedging the sector exposure with the ETF.
Why exclude interest on cash?
A market-neutral book holds its capital in cash and earns the T-bill rate on it. Counting that interest as strategy return and then computing a Sharpe ratio without subtracting the risk-free rate overstates the strategy, and more so after 2022, when T-bills paid 4% to 5%. Here, interest lifted the full-period Sharpe from 0.05 to 0.21 for the main version after costs.
Would better execution have saved it?
Not in this test. Trading at the close instead of the next open, with a 3 bp penalty for market-on-close orders, raised the gross Sharpe in 2016 to 2020 but not in 2021 to 2025, and lowered it after costs in both halves. Gross of costs the second half was already near zero.
Is this investment advice?
No. It is a backtest of a published academic strategy on a hand-built universe, with known biases listed on this page.
